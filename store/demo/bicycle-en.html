A Short History of the Bicycle

6 min read

Two wheels, one idea

In 1817, a German inventor named Karl Drais pushed himself along a road on a wooden frame with two wheels and no pedals. People called it the running machine. It was heavy, it rattled, and it had no brakes, but it proved something important: a person could balance on two wheels in a line.

The boneshaker years

Pedals arrived in the 1860s, fixed directly to the front wheel. Riders pushed the wheel around with their feet, and the iron tyres on cobbled streets earned these machines a fitting name. Comfort was not yet part of the design.

Bigger wheels, bigger risks

To go faster without gears, makers simply made the front wheel larger. The high-wheeler could reach impressive speeds, but the rider sat far above the ground. A stone in the road could send them over the handlebars, a fall so common it had its own name.

The safety bicycle

In the 1880s, the chain drive changed everything. Power now went to the rear wheel, both wheels could be the same size, and riders sat low enough to put a foot down. It looked much like the bicycles we ride today, and sales grew quickly.

Air in the tyres

Pneumatic tyres soon followed. A cushion of air smoothed out rough roads and made longer journeys practical. Cycling clubs formed in many cities, and road maps were printed for riders long before they were printed for drivers.

Freedom on wheels

For many people, the bicycle was the first personal vehicle they could afford. It widened the distance between home and work, changed what people wore, and gave a new kind of independence to anyone who could ride.

Lighter and faster

Through the twentieth century, frames moved from steel to aluminium and later to carbon fibre. Gears multiplied, brakes improved, and racing bicycles became precise machines tuned for every gram.

Back in the city

Today the bicycle is returning to city streets as a quiet, clean way to travel. Shared bikes, protected lanes and electric assistance have brought it to people who had not ridden in years. The idea from 1817 is still rolling.
